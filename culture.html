Marriage — The Chak-Long Ceremony

A traditional Tai-Ahom marriage ceremony carrying promises, responsibility and cultural values.

Chak-Long is a traditional Tai-Ahom marriage ceremony that marks the beginning of a new family. The ceremony carries both social and religious importance, with the couple making their promises in the presence of parents, elders and the Mo'lung (priest).

Traditionally, the marriage ceremony was observed over several days, although it gradually became shorter. Important customs include Joron-Diya, where the bride receives wedding clothes and ornaments, and Pani-Tola, involving water brought for the ceremonial preparations.

On the main Chak-Long day, the bride wears traditional clothing and ornaments while the groom appears in traditional Tai-Ahom attire. The exchange of the Hengdang (sword) and Kavas-Kapor represents courage, protection, responsibility, skill and ability.

The priest guides the couple through the ceremony and gives advice for their life together. Rituals, prayers and symbolic acts remind the couple of their responsibilities towards each other, their families and their community.

“Chak-Long is not only a marriage ceremony, but a passage into a new life—carrying promises, responsibility and tradition from one generation to the next.”

After Death — Moidam & Remembrance

The Tai-Ahom tradition of honouring the dead and remembering ancestors.

Moidams are burial mounds associated with the Tai-Ahom Dynasty from the 13th to the 19th century CE. The Tai-Ahoms believed that although the physical body perishes, the spirit continues to live and should be remembered and honoured by descendants.

The practice of entombing the dead in Moidams is an important part of Tai-Ahom funerary tradition. The living commemorate their ancestors through Tarpan, the offering of consecrated water, and Me-Dam-Me-Phi, a ceremony of ancestor worship.

Moidams can be found at several locations across the Upper Brahmaputra valley. Among them, Charaideo is especially significant because of its concentration and comprehensive grouping of Moidams. It remained a sacred centre of royal burial and religious activity.

After the death of Siu-ka-Pha in 1268 CE, he was buried at Charaideo according to traditional Tai-Ahom rituals. Later Ahom kings continued this burial tradition, keeping Charaideo connected with the memory and spiritual heritage of the Tai-Ahom people.

Remembering the ancestors is not only about the past—it keeps a living connection between generations.

Martial Arts in Dance — Hengdang Nritya

Where traditional martial movements become a powerful form of cultural expression.

Kaa Nap Cheng, also known as Hengdang Nritya or Sword Dance, is one of the prominent examples of martial arts incorporated into Tai-Ahom dance traditions. It is generally performed by two male dancers using the Hengdang sword.

The dancers perform carefully coordinated movements that simulate combat. These include offensive strikes, defensive parries, footwork and transitions between attack and defence. The performance requires strength, agility, precision, discipline and mental focus.

Traditional instruments such as the Kong (drum), Kong Cheng (big drum), Paiseng (cymbal) and Jaamtong (gong) provide the rhythm of the performance. The music guides the speed and intensity of the dancers' movements.

Beyond physical skill, the sword dance expresses ideas of balance, control and harmony between mind and body. Through dance, martial traditions that once formed part of warfare are preserved as cultural expression and passed on to younger generations.

Through movement, rhythm and discipline, the sword dance keeps an ancient martial heritage alive.
